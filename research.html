30 Years of Welfare-to-Work: Intergenerational Impacts of Welfare Reform

Job market paper · Draft coming soon

Nearly 30 years ago, President Bill Clinton signed the Personal Responsibility and Work Opportunity Reconciliation Act (PRWORA), pushing almost three million families out of welfare and into the labor force. Using internal Census Bureau data, we document the long-run impacts of PRWORA on individuals who were children at the time of reform. Our design compares siblings born to the same mother with differential exposure to reform and we find that childhood exposure to welfare reform increases both adult earnings and employment. These effects follow a clear life-cycle trend, appearing as early as age 18 and fading — rather than compounding — with age. Employment is concentrated in lower-paying, less stable jobs, consistent with children internalizing the “work first” mantra of welfare reform and prioritizing employment at the expense of job quality. We additionally find that welfare reform reduces the incidence of teen birth, but has mixed impacts on safety net participation. Effects are more pronounced for girls than boys and proportional to parents’ time spent working, suggesting that a key mechanism behind our results is children emulating their parents.

The Take-Up Gap: Sorting versus Place Effects in Medicaid

with Carl McPherson

Geographic variation in Medicaid take-up could reflect the effect of place itself, or simply the sorting of different types of people into different places. Using internal Census Bureau data, we adapt an AKM-style decomposition from the worker-mobility literature to separate these two channels, obtaining a place and person effect of Medicaid take-up. We then investigate how migration to different locations impacts individual take-up rates, and demonstrate how take-up is influenced by specific state policy or individual characteristics.

Discretion and Disqualification in SNAP Work Requirements

I study the impact of SNAP work requirements on program participation and employment in California, using detailed caseworker-level administrative data from the California Department of Social Services (CDSS).

Does Welfare Reduce Mortality? Evidence from Supplemental Security Income

Undergraduate honors thesis, Stanford University · Advisor: Mark Duggan

In this paper, I test whether transfer payments from the Supplemental Security Income (SSI) program reduce infant mortality in U.S. counties. In its 1990 Sullivan v. Zebley decision, the Supreme Court relaxed medical eligibility criteria for children on SSI, resulting in a dramatic rise in child enrollment and a large influx of income into some of the poorest areas in the U.S. I use an instrumental variable strategy, exploiting geographic variation in the expected treatment effect of this policy change, to test whether the resulting increase in child SSI enrollment had a causal impact on infant mortality. I find that a 1 percentage point increase in child SSI enrollment leads to a 1.45 percentage point reduction in infant mortality rates.
