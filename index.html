I am a Ph.D. candidate in the Economics Department at UC Berkeley. I study the impact of safety net programs on the economic security, mobility, and long-run outcomes of low-income households. My work combines administrative data with quasi-experimental methods to understand how program design and eligibility rules shape who benefits from anti-poverty programs. I am on the job market in 2026–27.

Before joining Berkeley, I worked as a research assistant in the Princeton Program in Public Finance. I hold a bachelor's degree in Economics from Stanford University.

Job market paper

30 Years of Welfare-to-Work: Intergenerational Impacts of Welfare Reform

Job market paper · Draft coming soon

Nearly 30 years ago, President Bill Clinton signed the Personal Responsibility and Work Opportunity Reconciliation Act (PRWORA), pushing almost three million families out of welfare and into the labor force. Using internal Census Bureau data, we document the long-run impacts of PRWORA on individuals who were children at the time of reform. Our design compares siblings born to the same mother with differential exposure to reform and we find that childhood exposure to welfare reform increases both adult earnings and employment. These effects follow a clear life-cycle trend, appearing as early as age 18 and fading — rather than compounding — with age. Employment is concentrated in lower-paying, less stable jobs, consistent with children internalizing the “work first” mantra of welfare reform and prioritizing employment at the expense of job quality. We additionally find that welfare reform reduces the incidence of teen birth, but has mixed impacts on safety net participation. Effects are more pronounced for girls than boys and proportional to parents’ time spent working, suggesting that a key mechanism behind our results is children emulating their parents.
